Position: Housing & Parking

Parking and Development: Plan Ahead, Protect Neighborhoods

By Tiffany Boccieri, candidate for Laguna Hills City Council, District 4 · Updated October 22, 2026

Parking is a daily, practical concern for residents. When a new project opens without enough spaces for its residents, the surrounding streets can feel the impact. I support meeting our housing obligations, but I also want Laguna Hills to use every reasonable, lawful tool it has to plan for parking before a problem reaches someone's block.

What the city can control

California housing law limits a city's ability to deny or delay qualifying housing developments. The Housing Accountability Act protects projects that comply with applicable objective standards, and other state laws limit parking minimums for certain projects. We cannot promise to stop an otherwise lawful housing project simply because neighbors are concerned about parking.

We have tools of our own, too. Laguna Hills' Housing Element received state certification in May 2024, and the city has adopted objective design standards for multifamily and mixed-use projects. Keeping those rules current, and applying them consistently, helps us avoid costly legal battles after an application arrives. Some projects also involve discretionary approvals or development agreements, but that authority is still subject to state law.

Get the parking facts independently checked

For major projects, I want the city to have reliable information about existing spaces, actual local demand, guest parking, and possible spillover onto nearby streets. The Village at Laguna Hills has a developer-prepared parking analysis among its published project documents. That is an important starting point, and the city can examine it further.

Where the law permits, I would propose city-selected independent peer review, paid for through lawful project-review fees or developer reimbursements. There is local precedent: for the Village, the city required the developer to pay for staff time, consultant fees, and required studies. City staff and the council should be able to question the assumptions behind a finished report. The goal is a fair, factual understanding of what the project will mean for neighboring streets.

Put practical rules in place before projects arrive

I would ask city staff to review which of our current parking, access, and parking-management requirements can be stated as clear, measurable rules under current state law. That includes guest-parking management and realistic enforcement where those requirements are legally available. The city should also publish a straightforward map explaining where state transit-related parking restrictions apply.

A citywide or area-wide parking study deserves consideration, especially where state Density Bonus Law allows substantial local evidence to support certain parking-ratio findings. That exception is limited, however: state law requires the city to pay for a new study used for that purpose. We should first identify the specific decisions the study could inform, estimate its cost, and then decide whether the public benefit justifies the expense.

Help neighborhoods respond when parking spills over

State law allows cities to create residential permit parking, and Laguna Hills already has those tools. In September, the city moved forward with permit restrictions on selected streets around Beckenham Park; permits were reported at $25 a year. The discussions also showed the downside of a rushed solution: restrictions on one street can push cars onto the next.

I'd like the city to make the petition process easier to understand, review complaints more promptly, and check the results after restrictions go into effect. That includes hearing from renters and residents as well as homeowners and HOAs. Any changes should spell out enforcement costs and who pays them, so that every cost stays visible to the public.

My commitments as your District 4 representative

  1. Independent scrutiny of major parking analyses, using city-selected reviewers and lawful cost recovery when available.
  2. Clear, legally supportable parking and management standards adopted before projects are proposed.
  3. A practical neighborhood response to overflow parking, with clear petitions, fair implementation, and follow-up checks for displaced parking.
  4. A current, compliant Housing Element, so Laguna Hills protects its legitimate local authority and keeps taxpayer money away from avoidable litigation.

Where is parking getting difficult?

Tell me the street, the time of day, and what you're seeing. That kind of detail helps the city base its response on what is actually happening.
