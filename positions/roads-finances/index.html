Position: Roads & City Finances

Fix Roads Before Small Problems Get Expensive

By Tiffany Boccieri, candidate for Laguna Hills City Council, District 4 · Updated October 22, 2026

I think responsible budgeting means doing the work while the repair bill is still small. Roads are a good example: regular maintenance is much less expensive than rebuilding pavement after it fails. My goal is to fund routine street care reliably, look hard at major contracts, and improve the city's finances without asking residents to pay new taxes.

Why maintenance matters

California's 2026 statewide streets assessment shows just how different the costs can be. It found that reconstructing a road can cost up to 11 times more than preserving it. Its listed major-road treatment costs range from $9.15 per square yard for preventive maintenance to $107.20 for reconstruction. Those are general statewide estimates, and they make the basic point: waiting can cost substantially more.

Laguna Hills has invested in preventive pavement maintenance and already resurfaces local streets zone by zone on a rotating cycle. But the city's 2026 pavement plan, adopted June 23, 2026, warns that at current funding the backlog of needed repairs will grow 265% by fiscal year 2032, and the Moulton Parkway overlay was deferred "due to lack of funding" in the adopted 2025–27 budget and has no construction funding in the current two-year period. The practical question for the next council is how to keep streets in reasonable condition before they move into a more expensive category.

Use reserves with care

The city has savings to build on. Its pre-audited figures for June 30, 2026 show about $22.53 million in the General Fund, and the audit is not yet complete; the last audited balance, at June 30, 2025, was about $23.15 million. Staff projects about $19.7 million by June 30, 2027, and the budget for that year includes about $5.7 million of transfers into capital projects. Those dollars help protect the city against emergencies, revenue downturns, and obligations we cannot predict, so spending from them requires a careful reason.

I would ask staff to bring the council a multi-year road plan showing the recommended work, the existing street-repair budget, committed and eligible transportation funds, and the amount of any remaining shortfall. If a limited, one-time use of available reserves would prevent significantly more expensive repairs, it should be considered, but only within the city's reserve policy and with a plan to replenish what is spent, consistent with recognized public-finance practice. Ongoing maintenance cannot depend on dipping into savings year after year.

Look closely at our largest obligations

The sheriff's contract, about $12.1 million of roughly $28.8 million in adopted FY 2026–27 General Fund spending, is the city's largest single operating cost. We should benchmark its costs, staffing, response, and results against appropriate neighboring contract cities, and check whether the cheapest agreement would provide the service we need. Routine maintenance and professional-services contracts should be competitively procured when the law, market, and procurement rules make that practical.

CalPERS' most recent valuation of the city's pension plan, as of June 30, 2025, shows an unfunded liability of about $6.08 million, with the plan 79.4% funded. That is a long-term obligation paid down through required annual payments, about $644,000 this fiscal year, and no $6.08 million bill comes due this year. Any proposal to make additional payments should be compared with road, emergency-reserve, and other needs using a current actuarial and financial analysis.

I'll also support a public long-range forecast that shows how operating revenues cover operating costs, and what happens under a weaker economy.

Make transportation funding work harder

We should pursue eligible Measure M2 and state transportation dollars and keep our local eligibility requirements current. Grants matter, and a stable city plan for street maintenance has to be the foundation. I would also support thoughtful commercial revitalization that strengthens the tax base while counting only revenue we actually receive.

Be realistic about the Village

The council adopted the amended development agreement for the Village at Laguna Hills on August 25, 2026, following the July public review. The current plan includes housing, hotels, and retail on the former mall property. A city-commissioned fiscal analysis estimates an annual General Fund surplus of approximately $1.40 million at full buildout, using FY 2026–27 budget figures: about $3.83 million in revenues less $2.43 million in expenses. The former mall has already been demolished, but no construction start date has been announced. The developer says site-development and building permits take roughly 6 to 12 months after approval.

That could make a meaningful contribution, but the estimate depends on what is actually built, when it opens, occupancy, revenue, and city costs. We should publish plain-language updates on construction progress and compare actual results with the assumptions as the project develops, and wait for the money to arrive before we spend it.

My commitments as your District 4 representative

  1. A realistic, multi-year street-maintenance plan, prioritizing cost-effective repairs and avoiding new taxes.
  2. Clear rules for any one-time use of reserves, within the reserve policy and with a replenishment plan.
  3. Regular value-for-money reviews of large contracts, pension funding options, and the city's long-range operating budget.
  4. Transparent reporting on the Village and other major development, so projected economic benefits are checked against actual results.

Which streets need attention?

Tell me what you're seeing: potholes, worn pavement, hard-to-cross streets, or a location that floods. I'll use those reports to ask better questions about the maintenance plan.
